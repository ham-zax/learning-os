## Ad-hoc dialogue replays (hypothetical next replies)

### T1 — Trigonometry: changed question after understanding coordinates

**Synthetic input:** Learner has established that unit-circle sine is vertical position and cosine horizontal position. Learner now asks, “Why name them at all instead of just x/y?”, and says a previous answer added too little novelty.

**A1-authored sample reply:**

> Yes, for a **single point** you can call its coordinates x and y. The names become useful when the point **moves with an angle**: sine and cosine are the rules that turn an angle into those coordinates. In a rotated drawing there may be many x's and y's, but `cos θ` still identifies the horizontal component of an angle θ, and `sin θ` its vertical component. That lets us write a rotation of any point as `x' = x cos θ − y sin θ`, `y' = x sin θ + y cos θ`; the same functions then describe triangle ratios and periodic motion/waves. So x and y name positions, while sine and cosine name reusable **relationships to angle**. We can keep using x and y when discussing one fixed drawing.

**Audit:** Directly answers the **new why-name question**, keeps the previously understood coordinate relation in one clause, provides three distinct uses (function of angle, rotation, triangles/waves) without first redefining sine/cosine or launching a full quiz. Its formula adds useful novelty at the cost of density; a fresh model/human may judge whether one formula is too much. **Rubric verdict for this sample: fits intent, short enough, not independently observed.** No state creation is warranted.

### T2 — Meditation: self-reported basics and request for practice

**Synthetic input:** Learner first requested first principles, then says “I know the basics. Give me a quick refresher and then practice,” preferring a faster pace.

**A1-authored sample reply:**

> Brief recap: pick something you can notice (often the breath), notice when attention has moved elsewhere, and return without treating the wandering as failure. The exercise is not to stop thoughts; the useful action is noticing and returning. Now practice for one minute: feel three natural breaths without controlling them, then keep the breath in awareness. Each time you notice a thought, sound or bodily feeling has carried you away, quietly identify that as a distraction and return to the next breath. At the end, tell me what most often drew attention away; we'll use that to choose the next explanation or exercise *in this conversation*, not to assign a score.

**Audit:** A true **recap then immediate usable practice** in one turn; does not repeat first principles at length, introduce an unwanted foundational diagnostic, call low attention a failure, or promote anecdotal reflection to durable evidence. In an ad-hoc discussion without active assessable state it remains ephemeral. **Rubric verdict for this sample: fits intent, not independently observed.**
